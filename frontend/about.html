About Banpani

A free, community-run map for coordinating relief during a disaster. This page explains what Banpani is, who it is for, how it works, and how it protects the people who use it.

Banpani is a free website for coordinating relief during a disaster. It puts needs, offers of help, hazards, and the state of the ground onto one shared and live map, so that help reaches the right places and effort is not wasted.

1. Overview

When a disaster strikes, relief is usually generous but disorganised. A great deal of it lives inside scattered messaging groups, where a request for medicine can be buried under a hundred later messages, and where two convoys can unknowingly travel to the same village while a third village waits. Banpani exists to fix that. It shows, on a single map that everyone can see at once, where help is needed, who is providing it, which roads are blocked, which shops and clinics are open, where the hazards and the safe routes are, and which places have been reported but not yet reached.

Banpani began in 2026 during the floods in Assam, India. It was built by volunteers, for the people affected, and it was designed from the start to be reused for the next disaster, anywhere. It is now general: it works for floods, fires, storms, earthquakes, disease outbreaks, industrial accidents, and more, in any country. There are no accounts, no logins, and no cost. Nobody owns it.

2. Who Banpani is for

  • People affected by a disaster, who can place a request for help on the map in under a minute, without registering.
  • Neighbours and bystanders, who can report what they can see near them.
  • Volunteers and relief groups, who can see the whole picture, avoid duplicating each other, and reach the places nobody has yet reached.
  • Non-governmental organisations, who can list themselves so that donors and coordinators can find them.
  • People who want to help in advance, who can sign up as standing volunteers to be contacted when a disaster happens near them.

Banpani is meant to be usable by an ordinary person on an ordinary phone, on a weak connection. It asks for as little as possible and never requires you to create an account.

3. What you can do

Every disaster page carries a toolkit suited to that kind of disaster. Depending on the situation, you can:

  • Report a need. Drop a pin, say what is needed (for example a boat, drinking water, medicine, or shelter), and roughly how many people are affected.
  • Offer help. Mark what you can provide, so that people who need it can find you. This is the supply side of the map.
  • Mark blocked roads. Show which routes are cut or damaged, so relief can route around them.
  • Mark open and closed facilities. Show which shops, pharmacies, clinics, fuel stations, and water points are open right now. This matters most during outbreaks and long emergencies.
  • Mark hazards and safe routes. Show danger zones (a fire front, a flooded area, a gas plume) and the escape routes away from them.
  • See the gaps. The map highlights places that have been reported but that nobody has yet covered, so effort goes where it is missing.
  • Add photographs as proof. A photo of the situation, a need, or completed relief work. The location information stored inside a photo is removed before it is uploaded.

4. How coordination pages work

Each disaster gets its own coordination page with its own live map and its own toolkit. These pages are created automatically. Nobody has to apply, and nobody approves them in advance.

When a person files the first report for a place, Banpani either attaches it to a nearby ongoing response of the same kind, or, if there is none, starts a new one. A brand new response appears on the map straight away, marked as unconfirmed, so that a genuine first report is never invisible. As more people report and confirm, the response becomes established.

To prevent misuse, a single device can only start a limited number of new responses in a day, and anyone can flag a response that is fake or duplicated. A response stays live for as long as the disaster is active. When it is over, it winds down and moves into a permanent public archive, so that the record of what happened, and when, is never lost.

5. How trust and verification work

There is no gatekeeper and no authority deciding what is true. The community checks each other's reports. The idea is simple: it takes a few ordinary people agreeing, not an official stamp, to make information trustworthy.

  • A report appears on the map straight away, marked unconfirmed.
  • When three different people confirm it, it becomes confirmed.
  • If people flag a report as false, it is hidden.
  • Groups and NGOs receive a community-verified mark after five people vouch for them.
  • Anyone can flag a fake or duplicate response, and a few flags will hide it.

Every action taken on Banpani is visible in a public activity log, with no names, no phone numbers, and no network addresses, shown only as an anonymous identifier for each person. There is also a public situation report that lists what is still unconfirmed and where the coverage gaps are. This is how the system stays honest and open at the same time.

6. Moderation and safety

Banpani is built to be hard to abuse and safe to use.

  • Reports and responses can be flagged by the community and are hidden once enough people agree they are false or duplicated.
  • Creating new responses is rate-limited per device, so one person cannot flood the map.
  • A maintainer can remove clearly harmful content that the community has not yet caught.
  • By deliberate choice, Banpani does not host categories that can put people in danger, such as tracking named missing persons, or mapping conflict, refugees, and other situations where publishing a location could get someone targeted. It is a tool for coordinating relief, not for locating individuals.

7. Privacy and your data

  • There are no accounts, no passwords, and no logins.
  • Phone numbers are never shown publicly. A number is revealed one at a time, only to a person who is actually coordinating help, and every reveal is recorded.
  • Network (IP) addresses are hashed. They are never stored or shown in a readable form. They exist only to detect abuse.
  • Everything else that people add, such as needs, hazards, and photographs, is published as open data that anyone can reuse.
  • Money is never collected or displayed anywhere on Banpani.

The full details are in the privacy policy, including what is kept, for how long, and your rights over it.

8. Disasters we cover

Banpani groups disasters into families, and gives each family the tools and the vocabulary that suit it:

  • Water: floods, flash floods, and related events.
  • Fire: wildfires and large urban fires.
  • Storm and wind: cyclones, hurricanes, and severe storms.
  • Geological: earthquakes and landslides.
  • Climate extreme: drought and heatwaves.
  • Health: disease outbreaks and pandemics, where the focus is on medicines, oxygen, hospital beds, and which shops and clinics are open.
  • Industrial: chemical and gas leaks.
  • Infrastructure: building collapses and major failures.
  • Agriculture: pest and livestock emergencies.

9. Languages

Banpani currently works in English, Assamese (অসমীয়া), and Hindi (हिन्दी). It chooses your language automatically from your browser's settings, and you can change it from the top of the map at any time. More languages can be added, and help translating is welcome. If you would like to add a language, please get in touch through the contact details below.

11. Open source and open data

Banpani's software is open source under the MIT License. Anyone can read it, improve it, fork it, or run it for the next disaster. The source code is public at github.com/parthajy/banpani.

The information that people add to Banpani, such as needs, hazards, listings, and photographs, is dedicated to the public domain under Creative Commons Zero (CC0 1.0), so that anyone can reuse it freely. Personal identifiers, such as phone numbers and network addresses, are stripped from all of it.

12. Governance, ownership and money

Banpani belongs to no company and no single person. It is run by a volunteer, together with a community of contributors, and it is meant to be given away, not held. Open licensing is what guarantees this: the community can always use, change, and re-run Banpani for the next disaster, no matter who is operating the current website.

Banpani does not, and will not, collect or display money. There is no payment, no donation collection, no advertising, and no paywall anywhere on the service. It is not a business, and it is not for sale.

13. Limitations and disclaimer

Banpani is a community tool, not a replacement for official rescue. Reports come from the public and can be wrong, incomplete, or out of date. In an emergency, call your official emergency number first (112 in India and much of the world, or your local number). Do not rely on Banpani alone for a life-safety decision.

Because the information is crowd-sourced, it should be treated as a helpful shared picture, not as a guarantee. Always confirm critical details, and always defer to official instructions from emergency services and government authorities.

14. Accessibility and offline use

Banpani is designed to work on low-end phones and weak connections, which are common in a disaster. It is light on data. It can be installed to your home screen and opened like an app, and it will still open and show the last map you saw even when your connection drops. If you find something that is hard to use or read, please tell us so we can fix it.

15. How to help

  • Report accurately, and confirm other people's reports when you know they are true.
  • Sign up as a standing volunteer, so you can be contacted when a disaster happens near you.
  • Help translate Banpani into another language.
  • Improve the software or run it for a new disaster. The code is on GitHub.
  • Share Banpani with people and groups who are helping, so more effort lands on the map.

16. Contact

You can reach the volunteer behind Banpani by email at parthajy@gmail.com. You can also use the Contact button on the map to leave a message. No phone numbers are shown to anyone, at any time.
